Remote work isn't one thing. A software engineer in a fully distributed company, a nurse who handles scheduling from home two days a week and a freelancer working from a different city every month are all "remote", and they need very different setups. This guide covers the habits that hold up across all of them.

## Start with your work style, not your job title

Two people with the same role can need opposite remote setups. Someone who does their best work in long, quiet blocks (a [Cartographer](../styles/cartographer.html) or [Tinkerer](../styles/tinkerer.html)) usually loves remote work, because it removes interruptions. Someone who thinks out loud and draws energy from people (a [Collaborator](../styles/collaborator.html) or [Catalyst](../styles/catalyst.html)) can find a fully remote week isolating unless they build in live collaboration.

If you haven't yet, [take the Work Style Assessment](../quiz.html). It takes about five minutes and tells you which parts of remote work to lean into and which to design around.

## The four foundations

### 1. A dedicated place to work

The single most consistent advice across remote companies' public handbooks is to separate where you work from where you rest. It doesn't have to be a home office. It can be a corner of a room, a fold-down desk or a coworking membership two days a week. What matters is that sitting there means "work mode" — and leaving means you're done.

At minimum, aim for:

- A chair that supports your lower back and lets your feet rest flat on the floor
- A screen with its top edge at or slightly below eye level (use the [desk setup calculator](../tools/desk-setup.html))
- Light from the side rather than directly behind your screen
- A reliable internet connection, with a phone hotspot as backup

### 2. A predictable rhythm

Without a commute, days blur. A predictable rhythm replaces the structure the office used to give you. The most effective remote workers use some version of:

1. **A start ritual** — a short walk, making coffee, reviewing the day's plan. It's a "fake commute" that tells your brain work has begun.
2. **Focus blocks first** — put your most demanding work before meetings, when your attention is fresh.
3. **A communication window** — batch email and chat into two or three set times instead of reacting all day.
4. **A shutdown ritual** — write tomorrow's top three tasks, close every tab, and say (out loud if it helps) that you're done.

### 3. Written, asynchronous communication

Remote teams that work well default to writing. A clear written update can be read by six people in six time zones, searched later and doesn't interrupt anyone's focus. Good async messages:

- State what you need and by when in the first line
- Include the context so the reader doesn't need to ask follow-up questions
- Say what you'll do if you don't hear back ("If no objections by Thursday, I'll ship it")

See our full guide to [async communication](async-communication.html) for templates.

### 4. Visible output

In an office, people see you working. Remotely, they only see what you produce and share. That isn't a reason to perform busyness — it's a reason to make progress visible. A short end-of-week note listing what you shipped, what's blocked and what's next does more for your reputation than any number of green status dots.

## Common remote work problems and fixes

| Problem | What usually causes it | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Working until 9 p.m. | No clear end to the day | Fixed shutdown time, laptop out of sight |
| Feeling disconnected | Only transactional calls | One non-work chat per week; coworking days |
| Constant interruptions | Chat used for everything | Batch notifications; set status windows |
| Back or neck pain | Laptop on a kitchen table | External keyboard and a raised screen |
| Unclear priorities | No written goals | Weekly written priorities agreed with your manager |

## For managers of remote teams

Managing remotely is mostly about clarity. Teams struggle when expectations live in someone's head. The basics:

- **Write down what "good" looks like** for each role — outcomes, not hours.
- **Hold short, regular one-to-ones.** Fifteen minutes every week beats an hour every month.
- **Agree on response-time norms.** For example: chat within four working hours, email within one business day, urgent issues by phone.
- **Design for different work styles.** Not everyone needs the same number of meetings. The [team assessment](../teams.html) makes those differences visible so you can plan around them.

## Remote work and money

Remote work can save money — and cost it. Commuting, lunches and work clothes go down; heating, electricity, internet and furniture go up. Run your own numbers with the [commute savings calculator](../tools/commute-savings.html). Depending on where you live, some home-office costs may be tax-deductible or reimbursable by your employer, so check the rules in your country or ask an accountant.
