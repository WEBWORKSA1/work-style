Managing a Team With Different Work Styles

How to spot work-style differences on your team, reduce friction, and design meetings, workloads and schedules that fit everyone.

By the Work.Style editorial team · 10 min read · Updated October 2026

Most team friction isn't about skill or effort. It's about style. One person wants a plan before starting; another wants to start and figure it out. One wants to talk it through; another wants it in writing. Neither is wrong — but without naming the difference, it turns into frustration.

Step 1: Make styles visible

You can't manage what nobody has said out loud. Ask every team member to take the Work Style Assessment and share their result. Then spend 30 minutes as a team discussing:

  • What each person needs to do their best work
  • What drains them
  • One thing they'd like teammates to know about how they work

Larger teams can request a team report that maps everyone on one page.

Step 2: Find your team's natural tensions

The three Work.Style axes each create a predictable tension:

Axis Tension Typical friction
Deep ↔ Sprint Long focus vs quick bursts "Why does she take so long to reply?" vs "Why does he interrupt me all day?"
Independent ↔ Collaborative Alone vs together "Too many meetings" vs "Nobody talks to each other"
Planned ↔ Flexible Plan first vs start first "We keep changing direction" vs "We're stuck planning forever"

Name the tension, and most of the heat goes out of it.

Step 3: Design the team's rhythm

A team rhythm that works for everyone usually includes:

  • Protected focus time (for deep-focus styles), such as meeting-free mornings
  • Regular live collaboration (for collaborative styles), such as a weekly working session
  • A written plan with room to adapt (for both planners and improvisers): fixed goals, flexible methods
  • Async updates so nobody has to chase status

Step 4: Pair complementary styles

Some combinations make each other better:

  • Cartographer + Coordinator: the Coordinator protects the Cartographer's focus and handles stakeholders.
  • Tinkerer + Mentor: the Mentor brings just enough structure to finish what the Tinkerer starts.
  • Catalyst + Sprinter: sparks plus follow-through.
  • Collaborator + Cartographer: ideas turned into a plan.

Step 5: Adjust one-to-ones by style

  • Deep-focus styles: fewer, longer one-to-ones; send topics in advance.
  • Sprint styles: shorter, more frequent check-ins.
  • Independent styles: ask open questions; they won't always volunteer problems.
  • Collaborative styles: make time for thinking out loud together.
  • Planned styles: give clear expectations and advance notice of changes.
  • Flexible styles: give outcomes and constraints, not step-by-step instructions.

What not to do

  • Don't use work styles to label or limit people. Styles describe preferences, not abilities.
  • Don't use any assessment — ours included — for hiring, firing or promotion decisions. It isn't designed or validated for that.
  • Don't assume styles are fixed. People adapt, and preferences shift with role and life stage.

Run a team workshop

A 90-minute workshop is enough to start:

  1. 15 min: everyone shares their result
  2. 20 min: map the team on the three axes
  3. 25 min: discuss tensions and examples
  4. 20 min: agree three changes to how you work
  5. 10 min: set a date to review
