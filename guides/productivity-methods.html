9 Productivity Methods and Which One Fits Your Work Style

Pomodoro, time blocking, GTD, the Eisenhower matrix, Kanban and more — what each method is, how to start, and which work styles it suits.

There's no best productivity method. There's the method that fits how you work. A system built for someone who loves long focus sessions will frustrate someone who works in quick bursts, and the reverse. Here are nine of the most widely used methods, with who each one suits.

Quick match table

1. Pomodoro

Developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s and named after a tomato-shaped kitchen timer. Work for 25 minutes, take a 5-minute break; after four rounds, take a longer 15–30 minute break.

How to start: pick one task, open the focus timer, and do one round. Many people adjust the interval to 50/10 for deeper tasks.

Why it works: a short, fixed interval makes starting easier, and the breaks prevent fatigue.

2. Time blocking

Instead of a to-do list, you give every task a slot in your calendar. Your calendar becomes your plan for the day.

How to start: at the end of each day, block tomorrow — focus blocks first, then meetings, then a block for email and admin. Leave 20% of the day unscheduled for the unexpected.

Why it works: it forces you to be realistic about how much fits in a day.

3. Deep work

Popularized by computer science professor Cal Newport's 2016 book of the same name: protect long, distraction-free sessions for cognitively demanding work.

How to start: schedule one 90-minute block per day with notifications off and your phone in another room. Increase gradually.

Why it works: hard problems need sustained attention, and every switch costs time to regain focus.

4. Getting Things Done (GTD)

David Allen's method from his 2001 book. Capture every task and idea in a trusted inbox, then regularly process it: if it takes under two minutes, do it now; otherwise delegate it, defer it, or turn it into a "next action" on a context list.

How to start: do one "mind sweep" — write down every open loop in your head. Then process the list.

Why it works: your brain stops using energy to remember things.

5. The Eisenhower matrix

Sort tasks into four boxes:

Urgent Not urgent
Important Do now Schedule
Not important Delegate Drop

How to start: take today's list and put each item in a box. Most people discover they spend too long in "urgent, not important".

6. Kanban

Originated in manufacturing and adapted for knowledge work. Put tasks on a board with columns such as To do, Doing and Done, and limit how many cards can sit in Doing at once.

How to start: use sticky notes or any board tool. Set a work-in-progress limit of three.

Why it works: it makes bottlenecks visible and stops you starting more than you finish.

7. Eat the frog

Do your most important, most-avoided task first thing in the morning. Everything after that is easier.

How to start: before you finish today, write down tomorrow's frog.

8. Timeboxing

Give a task a fixed amount of time — say 45 minutes — and stop when the time is up, whether it's finished or not. Then decide whether it deserves another box.

Why it works: it prevents perfectionism and endless exploration. Great for Tinkerers.

9. The weekly review

Once a week, spend 30–60 minutes reviewing goals, clearing inboxes, checking your calendar and choosing next week's priorities.

How to start: Friday afternoon or Monday morning, same time every week. It's the habit that keeps every other method working.

Combine, don't collect

Most people end up with a combination: time blocking for the week, Pomodoro inside the blocks, and a weekly review to keep it all honest. Start with one method, use it for two weeks, then adjust. Switching systems every week is its own form of procrastination.
